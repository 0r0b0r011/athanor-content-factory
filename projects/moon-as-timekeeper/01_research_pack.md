# Moon as Timekeeper — Research Pack

## Concept

The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half from where we stand. These changing shapes are the lunar phases. One full cycle of phases (new moon to new moon) takes about 29.5 days. This is the **synodic month** [1].

The Moon's orbit itself, measured against the stars, takes about 27.3 days. This is the **sidereal month**. The phase cycle is about two days longer because Earth is also moving around the Sun. The Moon has to travel a little further to get back to the same Sun–Earth–Moon arrangement [1][2].

### Core facts (for the creator, not the child)

- Sidereal month (one orbit relative to the stars): 27.32 days. Synodic month (one cycle of phases): 29.53 days [1].
- The Moon is tidally locked: it rotates once per orbit, so the same face points toward Earth. (A slight wobble called libration means about 59% of the surface is visible over time.)
- Phase order: new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full → waning gibbous → third (last) quarter → waning crescent → new.
- The terminator is the line between day and night on the Moon.
- Orientation depends on the observer's latitude:
  - From mid-northern latitudes, the waxing Moon is lit on the **right**.
  - From the Southern Hemisphere it is lit on the **left**.
  - Near the equator the lit side is toward the bottom or top.
  - Any "right side = growing" rule in the content must say which hemisphere it is for, or the content must avoid the rule.
- Earth's shadow causes **eclipses**, not phases. A lunar eclipse can only happen at full moon.
- The Moon is often visible in daytime. A first-quarter moon rises around midday and is visible in the afternoon sky.

## Timekeeping connection

The project's title promises timekeeping, so the research must support it:

- The phase cycle is regular and easy to observe, which is why many cultures used it as a calendar.
- The English words "month" and "moon" share a root.
- Several calendars in use today are lunar (Islamic Hijri) or lunisolar (Hebrew, Chinese).
- For 6–8-year-olds, the useful claim is: "the Moon's shapes repeat in the same order, about once a month, so you can use them to count time."

**Gap:** `00_intent.yaml` has a mechanism learning goal ("explain why the moon appears to change shape"). It has no timekeeping goal ("use the phase pattern to predict or count days"). See Open questions.

## Age-band profile (6-8)

- Transition into Piaget's concrete operational stage, usually from around age 7. Treat stage boundaries as approximate.
- Can classify and seriate (put objects in order). Phase ordering is a seriation task, which suits this age.
- Understands cause and effect when it can be seen or handled.
- Egocentrism decreasing; can take simple perspectives. Imagining the view from the Moon or from space is still hard.
- Literal thinking; struggles with abstract models.
- Curious about natural phenomena.
- Can track a simple calendar or schedule.

## Misconceptions

Frequency labels below come from the cited studies. Most participants were older than this project's audience. Treat them as likely, not measured, for 6–8-year-olds.

- **"Earth's shadow causes the phases."** The most persistent alternative explanation. It is widespread enough that a large share of preservice elementary teachers held it before instruction [6][7]. The content must not show a shadow sweeping across the Moon.
- **"A cloud covers part of the moon."** A common naive explanation among children [7].
- **"The moon physically changes size or shape."**
- **"The moon only comes out at night."** Contradicted by everyday daytime-Moon sightings. Use them.
- **"The moon makes its own light."**

Note for QA: the eclipse/shadow idea is common among adults too. Facilitator materials (parent guide, teacher card) must state the correct model plainly so adults don't reintroduce the shadow explanation.

## Accurate model (age-appropriate)

- The Moon doesn't change shape. We see different amounts of its sunlit side.
- The Sun always lights half the Moon.
- As the Moon goes around Earth, we see different parts of that lit half.
- **Embodied model ("you are the Earth"):**
  - Darken a room and use one lamp as the Sun.
  - The child holds a ball (the Moon) at arm's length, a little above head height.
  - The child turns slowly in place, watching the ball.
  - The child's head is Earth.
  - As the child turns, the lit part of the ball grows from a sliver to a full circle and back.

  This is the standard light-source-and-ball phases model [4][5]. Do **not** describe it as "walking around a lamp": that models Earth going around the Sun, which does not cause phases.
- Phases repeat in the same order about every month, so they can be used to count time.

## Emotional hooks

- **Mystery:** the moon "sends different letters" each night.
- **Pattern-seeking:** children naturally love tracking patterns.
- **Agency:** predicting the next phase is empowering.
- **Competence:** being "someone who can read the sky".

## Symbolic opportunities

- **Phase wheel:** a physical dial the child turns. It doubles as a counting calendar.
- **Moon envelope:** a container for each phase.
- **Observation journal:** a track-and-record ritual. Include daytime sightings.

## Safety constraints

- Do NOT depict night as scary or dangerous.
- Do NOT suggest the moon causes bad things.
- Do NOT anthropomorphise the moon as lonely; it creates unnecessary melancholy.
- Keep illustrations warm, inviting, gentle.
- Night-observation tasks must be framed as done with an adult. Offer daytime-Moon alternatives so families without a safe outdoor space or a late bedtime can still take part. (Ethics policy: content must not assume access.)
